Five moves that make a roof problem cost more

  • Climbing up for a look.

    Ladder falls kill more than 100 people a year in the United States, NIOSH reports, and about 40 percent of cases involve the wrong angle for the ladder. Binoculars in the yard show a roofer nearly everything worth describing.

  • Letting a wet room dry on its own.

    The EPA's mold guide puts the advice briefly: act on water problems as soon as they turn up, and dry everything thoroughly.

  • Throwing out the evidence.

    New Jersey's insurance department asks homeowners to photograph damaged property before disposing of it and to keep receipts for temporary repairs.

  • Reaching for heat cable.

    UMass's building-science group says heat cable does not address the cause of an ice dam, which is heat escaping into the attic.

  • Walking toward a wire.

    The utility's advice is blunt: any downed line may be energized. Give it 30 feet or more, and dial 1-800-436-7734 for PSE&G, or 911 if anyone is close to it.

Patch it or replace it: three questions

How much of the roof is involved?

A guide quoted in the Department of Energy's repair brief puts the line at 10 percent of the old roof needing repair; past that, it recommends a new roof. One slope with a handful of bad shingles is a repair. Damage on every slope is a different question.

Is the trouble in one place or several?

Water that returns to the same spot after a patch points to a flaw nearby, often flashing, and can be dealt with narrowly. Stains in new places every season say the covering as a whole is giving up.

What is under the shingles?

Wet decking and soaked insulation change the answer, since the same brief says soaked insulation has to come out. A roofer who has seen the attic side can say which of the three situations a roof is in.

Which job comes up when

Winter

December to February

Teterboro's January averages 32.8 degrees, with about 16 days that freeze overnight and thaw by afternoon. That is ice dam season, and the time when flashing around a chimney shows the gaps it was hiding: ice dams and chimney and flashing.

Spring

March to May

The thaw shows what winter did. Stains appear on ceilings, and a walk around the house turns up lifted shingles and sagging gutters: roof inspection and roof repair.

Summer

June to August

The three wettest months at Teterboro, and the month Isaias arrived in 2020. Flat sections and gutter corners take the downpours, and wind brings limbs down on shingles: flat roofs and storm damage.

Autumn

September to November

Ida arrived on 1 September 2021. Leaves fill valleys and gutters before the first freeze, and it is a good moment to look at a full job before winter: gutters and downspouts and roof replacement.
