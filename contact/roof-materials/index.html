Shingles, slate, metal and membranes

Roof materials, one covering at a time

A plain guide to the coverings found on roofs in North Arlington, Kearny, Lyndhurst and Belleville, drawn from federal agencies, the National Park Service and the asphalt roofing trade group. It describes what those pages say, not what any one roofer installs.

Asphalt shingles, the common covering

The National Park Service's roofing brief notes that asphalt shingles date from the 1890s and are common substitutes for wood, slate and tile. FEMA's fact sheet on asphalt shingles in high-wind regions asks for a starter strip at the eave, six nails per shingle there, roofing nails rather than staples, and underlayment every time, with a check that the tabs have sealed after the sun has warmed them.

The Department of Energy's cool-roof page says to choose light or cool-colored granules on asphalt shingles, and advises against field-coating existing shingles.

Slate and clay tile: heavy and brittle

The Park Service brief says top-grade slate with good flashing will last 40 to 60 years with minimal maintenance, and that soft slates erode and delaminate. Winter ice and ice dams are a major cause of breakage. Slate and tile cannot support much weight, so the brief advises against walking on them and suggests a ladder hung over the ridge or planks spanned across the roof.

Tiles crack when struck by branches or by foot traffic, and under-fired tiles craze and spall in freeze and thaw. The Department of Energy lists slate as usually not a cool roofing material.

Wood shingles and shakes

According to the Park Service brief, wood shingle faces erode from rain and ultraviolet light, and prolonged moisture lets moss or fungi grow, which leads to rot. Shingles wider than about 6 inches tend to split when walked on. The brief asks for inspection at least twice a year.

The Department of Energy describes wood shingles and shakes as naturally cool-colored.

Metal: pitting, streaks and mismatched metals

The Park Service brief lists what goes wrong with sheet metal: pitting and streaking from pollutants, galvanic corrosion where dissimilar metals touch, rust once an iron coating is worn, and fatigue at joints and protrusions. A galvanized coating protects iron only while it is intact.

On metal shingles and tiles, the Department of Energy recommends a light or cool paint applied at the factory, calls unpainted metal a poor choice, and says oven-baked factory paint holds up better than paint applied in the field.

Flat roof coverings

In the Department of Energy's terms, a roof pitched at 2:12 or flatter is low-slope. Its usual coverings include spray polyurethane foam, modified bitumen, built-up roofs and single-ply membranes, each with its own reflective options and each installed differently.

The Asphalt Roofing Manufacturers Association's page on ponding water gives 1/4 inch of fall per foot as the minimum slope, and counts water that is still standing after 48 hours as ponding. It names drains and scuppers as the exits and notes that freeze and thaw can scrub a membrane where water sits.

The layer under the layer

The Department of Energy's repair brief describes underlayment as one layer of 30-pound felt with a 2-inch overlap, or two layers of 15-pound felt. FEMA's fact sheet says to use underlayment in every case.

The Building America guide to ice dam prevention adds self-adhering membranes around eaves, valleys, dormers and chimney penetrations, the spots where water is most likely to be pushed under a covering.

Light roofs, dark roofs and a cold winter

According to the Department of Energy, the surface of a standard roof can pass 150 degrees on a sunny summer afternoon, while a reflective roof stays more than 50 degrees cooler. Winter reverses part of the trade: a cool roof absorbs less sunlight and can add to heating needs, so in colder climates the energy cost may rise if that penalty outweighs the cooling savings. The page adds that cool roofs may be more prone to condensation than dark ones in cold climates, which proper design can prevent.

Teterboro Airport's normals run from a July average high of 87.0 degrees to a January average low of 25.7, so both seasons count here. The DOE page says a cool roof does not necessarily cost more on a new or replacement roof.

  2. NOAA NCEI, 1991-2020 monthly climate normals, Teterboro Airport, NJ (station USW00094741)

Choosing between two materials

A roofer can compare what two coverings would mean for a particular house: the weight on the frame, the pitch, the flashing details and the layers underneath.
